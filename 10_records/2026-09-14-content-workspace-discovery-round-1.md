# Content Workspace — Discovery Round 1

Date: 2026-09-14
Status: ACTIVE / INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE
Lifecycle stage: Stage 1 — Discovery
Case: Content Factory — End-user Content Workspace / text-source vertical slice

## Purpose

Test the Stage 0 product hypothesis without treating the existing implementation as proof of user need.

The discovery question is not whether Content Factory can turn one source into many formats. That capability is already represented by mature products and therefore is not, by itself, a differentiated problem statement.

The current discovery target is the decision layer between source material and production:

`SOURCE → what is worth making? → which independent story/angle? → PRODUCTION`

The working hypothesis is that users may experience the editorial selection and evidence-grounding step as a meaningful bottleneck that is insufficiently solved by generic repurposing/reformatting tools.

This remains a hypothesis.

## External evidence reviewed

GOV.UK Discovery guidance states that discovery should establish who users are, what they are trying to do, how they currently do it, and the problems/frustrations they experience before planning, designing or building. It recommends interviews, observation, existing evidence and current-experience mapping, and explicitly warns against treating assumptions as established needs.

GOV.UK also recommends turning assumptions into research questions, prioritising them, and using small research rounds; for interviews/contextual research, 4–8 participants per round is a typical starting point. Discovery should also consider alternatives to building and estimate the cost/scale of the current problem where possible.

Market evidence reviewed separately shows that source-to-many-format repurposing is already a mature category. Existing products automate extraction, adaptation, formatting and distribution. Therefore a generic "repurpose one source into many assets" problem is not sufficient evidence for a new product direction.

## Current hypotheses

| ID | Hypothesis | Current status |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | Users need one substantive source converted into multiple formats. | SUPPORTED AS MARKET NEED; not differentiated |
| H2 | Manual production/reformatting is a significant time cost. | PLAUSIBLE; heavily automated already |
| H3 | The harder remaining problem is deciding what is worth making and choosing an editorial angle. | UNCONFIRMED |
| H4 | Users need independent stories/angles rather than simple format variants. | UNCONFIRMED |
| H5 | Evidence traceability from output → story → source reduces review/correction effort. | UNCONFIRMED |
| H6 | A story-first workflow is preferable to direct source → generation for at least one important user group. | UNCONFIRMED |
| H7 | There is a user segment with sufficiently frequent/high-cost pain to justify a dedicated product. | UNKNOWN |

## Current user groups to investigate

Do not create personas yet. These are recruitment hypotheses only:

1. Solo creator / expert producing content from long-form source material.
2. Content marketer or social/content manager adapting substantive material across channels.
3. Editor/content strategist responsible for deciding which ideas become publishable pieces.
4. Agency/content team handling repeated source-to-many-output workflows.

The research should determine whether these are materially different workflows rather than assume they are distinct personas.

## Required research protocol for the next round

Use recent real episodes, not feature opinions or hypothetical questions.

For each participant, reconstruct one concrete content-production episode from source acquisition to publication/delivery:

1. What was the source?
2. What outcome were they trying to achieve?
3. What did they do first?
4. Which tools/documents/people were involved?
5. Where did they decide what was worth making?
6. What alternatives or ideas did they reject, and why?
7. Where did they manually extract, rewrite, verify, or restructure meaning?
8. Where did work stop, wait, or get repeated?
9. What caused review/correction?
10. What was the cost in time, people, money, missed opportunities, or quality?
11. What workaround or existing product did they use?
12. What remained unsolved after using the existing tools?

Capture concrete artifacts where possible: source, brief, notes, outline, prompts, drafts, review comments, production checklist, published outputs and revision history. Do not ask participants to validate the Content Factory concept before reconstructing their existing workflow.

## Falsification tests

The hypothesis H3 should be weakened or rejected if research shows that:

- users mostly already know what to make and the dominant pain is execution/distribution;
- existing repurposing tools solve the meaningful bottleneck with acceptable quality;
- editorial selection is infrequent or low-cost;
- users do not distinguish stories/angles from format variants;
- traceability is not used during review or does not change correction effort;
- no identifiable user group has repeated, economically meaningful pain.

The hypothesis should become stronger only if repeated real episodes show a common decision bottleneck before production, meaningful cost/friction, inadequate current solutions, and a plausible improvement that is not merely another formatter/generator.

## Discovery decision boundary

No Product Problem/Opportunity or Product Decision is accepted by this record.

No implementation change is authorised by this round.

No new ontology/state/workflow primitive is justified by the current evidence.

The next legitimate evidence step is a bounded user-research round using 4–8 participants, followed by cross-case synthesis. If real participants cannot be accessed, continue desk research but mark the Discovery evidence as incomplete rather than substituting market evidence for user evidence.

## Current conclusion

`DISCOVERY ACTIVE / INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE`

The most important finding is a narrowing of the research target: generic content repurposing is already a mature capability category; the unresolved question is whether a distinct and valuable editorial-intelligence bottleneck exists between source material and production.
