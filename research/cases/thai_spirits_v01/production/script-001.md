# SCRIPT-001 — Why Thai spirit offerings coexist with Buddhism

Status: DRAFT / research-backed.

Opening:
Walk past a house, hotel, shop or office in Thailand and you may notice a small shrine outside the building. There can be flowers, drinks, food or incense in front of it.

The obvious question is: if Thailand is predominantly Buddhist, why are people making offerings to spirits?

The short answer is that Thai religious life does not fit neatly into a Buddhism-versus-spirits binary.

Research describes Thai ritual life as drawing from Theravada and Mahayana Buddhism, Brahmanism and spirit cults. These traditions can interact within the same social environment. [C001, C003]

The word phi — ผี — is also more complicated than the English word "ghost". Its meaning and classification have changed across historical and religious contexts. [C002]

One important example is the San Phra Phum, a spirit house associated with a guardian spirit or deity connected with a place. Research on Bangkok distinguishes Phra Phum, Jao Thii and Phra Phrom rather than treating every shrine as the same thing. [C004, C005]

Offerings can include food, drink, flowers, incense and other objects. In spirit-medium contexts, offerings and household altars can form part of an ongoing relationship between people and spirits. [C006, C007]

And this is not only a rural phenomenon. Research documents spirit-house practices and mediumship in urban Thailand as well. [C007, C009]

So the interesting part is not simply that "Thai people believe in ghosts".

It is that several religious traditions have developed alongside one another, and the boundaries between them can be more complicated than a Western category such as "religion versus superstition" suggests.

Thai religious life is layered, local and still changing. [C008, C009]

End.
