## 2. Editorial objective

Explain why different species can be similar for fundamentally different reasons: inheritance from common ancestry, persistence of conserved biological machinery, and independent recurrence of solutions under recurring functional or environmental problems. The piece must show why similarity has to be classified at the level being compared rather than assigned one global explanation.

## 3. Editorial question

**What is actually common between different species, and why do some similarities come from shared ancestry while others arise independently?**

The question should not be reframed into a claim that evolution has a single universal design space or deterministic set of solutions.

## 4. Narrative spine

1. **Life inherits a biological toolkit.**
   Begin with the distinction between superficial diversity and deep inherited biological machinery. Use C1–C3.

2. **Organisms repeatedly face functional and environmental problems.**
   Establish recurring selection pressures without implying that the same solution must result. Use C4.

3. **Constraints filter what is viable or accessible.**
   Explain that physics, ecology, development and inherited architecture can bias or restrict evolutionary trajectories. Present C5 explicitly as qualified rather than deterministic.

4. **Some solutions recur independently.**
   Use the locomotion example as the first concrete demonstration of convergence under physical/functional constraints. Use C5–C6.

5. **Similar outcome does not mean identical construction.**
   Use the camera-eye comparison between vertebrates and cephalopods to demonstrate phenotypic convergence while preserving differences in underlying architecture. Use C6–C7.

6. **Independent structures can reuse ancient components.**
   Explain why organ-level independence does not imply that every gene, regulatory component or developmental mechanism evolved independently. Use C8.

7. **Therefore similarity must be classified at the correct level.**
   End with C9: ancestry, phenotype, function, development and molecular components can carry different evolutionary relationships.

## 6. Required evidence-backed examples

### Example A — Locomotion

Use the independently recurring thunniform body plan across distantly related large aquatic vertebrates as evidence that recurring functional problems and physical constraints can contribute to recurring phenotypic solutions.

Required qualification: the example supports a bounded convergence mechanism; it does not establish that physics alone determines the outcome or that all lineages facing the same problem converge on one form.

Claim bindings: `C4`, `C5`, `C6`.

### Example B — Camera eyes

Use vertebrate and cephalopod camera eyes as a contrasting convergence example. The example must preserve the distinction between similar organ-level function/phenotype and different developmental/anatomical implementation.

Required qualification: independent organ-level evolution does not imply independent origin of every underlying component. Ancient genes or regulatory machinery may be reused.

Claim bindings: `C6`, `C7`, `C8`, `C9`.

## 7. Required conceptual distinctions

The content must explicitly distinguish:

- common ancestry vs. convergent evolution;
- inherited similarity vs. independently recurring phenotype;
- organ/phenotype-level convergence vs. component/gene-level homology or reuse;
- physical/ecological/developmental constraint vs. deterministic outcome;
- direct evidence vs. bounded synthesis;
- supported claims vs. qualified claims vs. unknowns.

## 8. Epistemic language requirements

Use probabilistic/bounded language where appropriate: `can`, `may`, `tends to`, `increases the probability`, `can constrain`, `can recur`.

Avoid deterministic formulations such as `must`, `forces`, `always`, `inevitably`, or equivalent wording unless a future claim-specific research pass establishes such a statement.

C5 must remain qualified throughout the content.

C10 must remain unknown. The specification may use it as a question or boundary, but not as an answer.

## 9. Forbidden editorial claims

The finished content must not assert or imply that:

1. constraints force the same evolutionary solution;
2. similar phenotype means common ancestry;
3. independent phenotype means every underlying component evolved independently;
4. convergent phenotype implies convergent genes as a general rule;
5. evolution searches a globally small fixed set of designs;
6. physics is the sole explanation for convergence;
7. the reviewed examples establish a universal law of evolutionary design.

## 10. Scope boundary

This specification is about explaining observed similarities across species using the bounded evidence represented in `RG-2026-09-14-001`.

It is not a general theory of evolutionary possibility, a complete survey of convergence, a quantitative model of evolutionary constraint, or a claim that the reviewed examples exhaust the mechanisms producing biological similarity.

If production requires a factual assertion not traceable to C1–C9, production must stop and return to research rather than fill the gap implicitly.
