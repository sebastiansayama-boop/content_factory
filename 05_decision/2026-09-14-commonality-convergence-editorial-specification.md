## 2. Editorial objective

Explain why different species can be similar for fundamentally different reasons: inheritance from common ancestry, persistence/reuse of conserved biological components, and independent recurrence of phenotypic solutions under recurring functional or environmental problems. The piece must show why similarity has to be classified at the level being compared rather than assigned one global explanation.

## 3. Editorial question

**What is actually common between different species, and why do some similarities come from shared ancestry while others arise independently?**

The question must not be reframed into a claim that evolution has a single universal design space or deterministic set of solutions.

## 4. Narrative spine

1. **Life inherits biological components.** Begin with the distinction between superficial diversity and deeply conserved cellular machinery. Use C1–C3. Avoid implying that every conserved component can be reconstructed directly to LUCA in its modern form.

2. **Organisms repeatedly face functional and environmental problems.** Establish recurring selection pressures without implying that the same solution must result. Use C4.

3. **Constraints can restrict or bias trajectories.** Explain that physics, ecology, development and inherited architecture can influence what is viable or accessible. Present C5 explicitly as qualified rather than deterministic.

4. **Some solutions recur independently.** Use thunniform locomotion as the primary example. The evidence is particularly strong for physical/hydromechanical demands contributing to convergence, but the story must not reduce the example to physics alone.

5. **Similar outcome does not mean identical construction.** Use vertebrate and cephalopod camera eyes. Describe the striking organ-level similarity while preserving major anatomical/developmental differences.

6. **Independent phenotype does not mean independent every-component origin.** Explain the camera-eye case as a mosaic: conserved developmental machinery can coexist with lineage-specific changes and recruitment. Use C7–C8.

7. **Therefore similarity must be classified at the correct level.** End with C9: ancestry, phenotype, function, development and molecular components can carry different evolutionary relationships.

## 6. Required evidence-backed examples

### Example A — Thunniform locomotion

Use the thunniform body plan across tunas, lamnid sharks, whales and derived ichthyosaurs as evidence that similar locomotor demands can be associated with independently recurring morphology and mechanics.

Required qualification: Motani's hydrodynamic model and later mechanical studies support strong physical/hydromechanical constraints in this example. They do not establish that physics alone determines the outcome or that every lineage facing comparable locomotor problems must converge on thunniform morphology.

Claim bindings: `C4`, `C5`, `C6`.

Primary external sources:
- Motani 2002, Nature 415:309–312.
- Donley et al. 2004, Nature 429:61–65.
- Motani & Shimada 2023, Scientific Reports 13:16664.

### Example B — Camera eyes

Use vertebrate and coleoid cephalopod camera eyes as a classic example of strong organ-level similarity/convergence.

Required qualification: The similarity does not imply that every underlying component evolved independently. Comparative developmental and transcriptomic work identifies conserved genes/components alongside lineage-specific changes and recruitment. Therefore the example should be presented as a multi-level/mosaic case rather than a binary "all homologous" versus "all independent" story.

Claim bindings: `C6`, `C7`, `C8`, `C9`.

Primary external sources:
- Yoshida & Ogura 2011, BMC Evolutionary Biology 11:180.
- Kröger et al. 2023, Current Biology 33:R1100–R1105.
- Allard & Kumar 2026, Nature Reviews Genetics 27:563–578.

## 7. Required conceptual distinctions

The content must explicitly distinguish:

- common ancestry vs. convergent evolution;
- inherited similarity vs. independently recurring phenotype;
- organ/phenotype-level convergence vs. component/gene-level homology, reuse or convergence;
- physical/ecological/developmental constraint vs. deterministic outcome;
- direct evidence vs. bounded synthesis;
- supported claims vs. qualified claims vs. unknowns.

## 8. Epistemic language requirements

Use probabilistic/bounded language where appropriate: `can`, `may`, `tends to`, `can constrain`, `can recur`, `is consistent with`, `contributes to`.

Avoid deterministic formulations such as `must`, `forces`, `always`, `inevitably`, or equivalent wording unless a future claim-specific research pass establishes such a statement.

C5 must remain qualified throughout the content.

C10 must remain unknown. It may be mentioned only as an unresolved broader hypothesis, not as a conclusion.

## 9. Forbidden editorial claims

The finished content must not assert or imply that:

1. constraints force the same evolutionary solution;
2. similar phenotype means common ancestry;
3. independent phenotype means every underlying component evolved independently;
4. convergent phenotype implies convergent genes as a general rule;
5. evolution searches a globally small fixed set of designs;
6. physics is the sole explanation for convergence;
7. vertebrate and cephalopod camera eyes are either wholly homologous or wholly independent at every biological level;
8. the reviewed examples establish a universal law of evolutionary design.

## 10. Scope boundary

This specification is about explaining observed similarities across species using the bounded evidence represented in `RG-2026-09-14-001` and the external sources registered there.

It is not a general theory of evolutionary possibility, a complete survey of convergence, a quantitative model of evolutionary constraint, or a claim that the reviewed examples exhaust the mechanisms producing biological similarity.

If production requires a factual assertion not traceable to C1–C9 or to a newly verified source, production must stop and return to research rather than fill the gap implicitly.
